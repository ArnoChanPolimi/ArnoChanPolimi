# Politecnico di Milano · Learning Distance

`M.Sc. Telecommunication Engineering · Milan, Italy · 2024–2026`

<img align="right" width="250" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/Polimi/Polimi_1stBuilding.jpg" alt="Politecnico di Milano">

After Beijing, the road turned west. I came to Milan carrying more than a suitcase. I carried the discipline of exam years, the memory of a laboratory at sunset, the uneasiness of a child from Gansu who had learned to move forward before learning how to feel at home.

At first I did not know whether to call Milan an adventure. The word sounded too clean. What I actually had was a practical courage: documents folded carefully, a route from the airport, a few words of Italian, and the private fear that distance might change me faster than I could understand.

The first days were made of small tests. A tram bell in the morning. A receipt in my pocket. The pause before ordering coffee. A street sign read once, then again. I was not only translating language. I was translating myself: the student I had been in Beijing, the son of a faraway province, the young engineer trying to stand inside Europe without pretending he had always belonged there.

<br clear="all">

<img align="left" width="250" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/Polimi/Duomo_Milano.jpg" alt="Duomo di Milano">

Milan did not answer these questions quickly. It did something better: it allowed them to remain open. I walked the same streets until their corners became familiar. I learned which light appeared after rain, which bakery opened early, which path to campus was kindest when my thoughts were heavy.

Belonging did not arrive like a door thrown open. It came as a slow adjustment of temperature. One day the city was foreign; another day it was still foreign, but less sharp. Then one morning I realized I knew where to stand on the platform before the train arrived. Such things sound small. They are not small when a person is rebuilding his map of the world.

Europe changed my sense of time. In Beijing, time often felt like a race: exams, deadlines, projects, the next gate. In Milan, time also had stone, shadow, evening light, old walls beside new machines. The Duomo did not make research easier. Lake Garda did not solve loneliness. The Alps did not write my thesis. Yet each of them quietly refused the idea that life should be reduced to productivity.

I have always been easily wounded by beauty, and just as easily steadied by it. A clean equation can do this. So can a roof at evening, or a line of trees, or the first glimpse of mountains when the mind had expected only buildings. Beauty does not make difficulty disappear. That would be too simple. It gives difficulty a place to breathe.

This matters to me because melancholy has always walked near my curiosity. I do not experience the world as a flat surface. Things enter deeply: a face on the tram, a blue hour over Milan, the silence after a failed experiment, the memory of my hometown when no one around me knows its name. For a long time I thought this sensitivity was only a weakness. Milan has taught me that it can also be an instrument, if I learn to tune it.

<br clear="all">

<img align="right" width="250" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/Polimi/GPU_set.jpg" alt="Thesis compute setup">

My research at PoliMi continues the same question that began at BIT, but on a wider map. In Beijing, beamforming taught me that communication is not only about sending power outward; it is about giving attention a direction. In Milan, the question grows larger. If Beijing taught me to listen to signals, Milan asks me to listen to systems: machines spread across geography, GPUs training large models, information moving through networks where distance is no longer a metaphor but a cost.

The problem is not beautiful in a decorative way. It is beautiful because its logic is humanly understandable. Many parts must act together. Each part has power, but none is sufficient alone. They compute, wait, exchange, synchronize, and begin again. A model learns only if distant machines can share what they have learned quickly enough. Intelligence, at this scale, is not the brilliance of one isolated unit. It is coordination.

That is why cross-geo communication interests me. It turns an abstract question into a practical one: how does knowledge move when the workers are far apart? What is lost in waiting? What can be rearranged so that expensive computation spends less of its life idle? I work with collective communication not because libraries are glamorous, but because they sit at the place where mathematics touches machines, and machines touch geography.

There is a philosophy hidden there, but not a distant one. It is the same lesson a person learns when crossing countries. No one remains whole by isolation. We become ourselves through exchange: imperfect, delayed, sometimes noisy, but necessary.

My English has changed here too. At first it was a room where I stood carefully, afraid to disturb the furniture. Now I move inside it with more ease. I argue, write, hesitate, correct myself, begin again. It has not erased the earlier rooms of my life. It has opened windows between them.

For now, Milan is where the road becomes spacious. Not simple. Spacious. I am still the person who carries weather inside him. But I am learning that sadness can be a form of attention, that attention can become work, and that work, if it is honest enough, can become a way of speaking back to the world.
