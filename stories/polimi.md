# Politecnico di Milano

`M.Sc. Telecommunication Engineering · Milan, Italy · 2024–2026`

<img align="right" width="250" src="../assets/photos/Polimi/Polimi_1stBuilding.jpg" alt="Politecnico di Milano">

I did not arrive in Milan with the confidence of someone beginning a grand adventure. I arrived with a suitcase, a few careful plans, and a kind of inward caution I had carried for years. A child from the mountains of Gansu does not learn to move through the world carelessly. He learns to count distance, to respect chance, to feel the weight of every door before he opens it.

My first life abroad began in that spirit: hopeful, alert, and a little afraid. I had imagined exile as something dramatic, but it came more softly. It was the pause before speaking in another language, the fatigue of reading every sign twice, the small loneliness of seeing beauty and having no one beside me who remembered where I had come from.

Milan answered with gentleness rather than spectacle. A tram bell in the morning. Coffee escaping from a bar before class. The old yellow light resting on wet stone after rain. A street that, after enough crossings, stopped being foreign and became part of my body’s knowledge. I began to understand that belonging is rarely announced. More often it gathers quietly, like dust in sunlight, until one day the room is yours.

Northern Italy has a way of making thought look up. On certain mornings the Alps appear at the end of an ordinary street, pale and still, like a theorem written on the horizon. Lake Garda opens so widely that the word "lake" feels almost too modest, as if language has brought a cup to the sea. The Duomo is never quite the same building twice. At noon it is stone. Toward evening it becomes a body of light.

I have always been vulnerable to beauty, almost embarrassingly so. A building, a cloud, a line of music, the clean shape of an equation: all of them can disturb me in the same quiet way. For a long time I thought this made me too melancholic, too easily moved. Milan has taught me to think otherwise. Beauty is not decoration. It is a form of attention. It says: the world is not only something to survive, or solve, or optimize. It is also something to behold.

PoliMi has shaped me in a quieter and more exacting way. Its best lessons are patient, almost severe. A result is not enough because it is correct; it must be understood. A system is not understood because it runs; one must know what assumption lets it run, what limit will make it fail, and what hidden price has been paid for its elegance. This is the part of engineering I love most: its refusal to be fooled by appearance.

My thesis sits where large AI systems meet communication networks. In distributed training, machines separated by distance must learn as if they shared one mind. They exchange gradients, wait for one another, lose time in the narrow passages where computation becomes communication. The task is concrete: make that exchange faster, waste less of the GPUs’ brief and expensive life. But beneath the task is a question I find almost philosophical: how can many separate parts act with the rhythm of one whole?

Perhaps that is also the question Milan has been asking me. How does a person made from one language, one childhood, one set of mountains and winters, enter a wider world without becoming less himself? My English, once something I wore awkwardly like a borrowed coat, has come closer to the skin. I study in it, argue in it, write in it, and sometimes catch myself thinking in it before thought has chosen a nationality. This has not made me rootless. It has given my roots more air.

I remain, by temperament, a little inward and often blue. But the sadness is no longer only a weight. Sometimes it is a lens. It lets me notice the fragile things: evening light on campus, the silence before an experiment begins, the strange dignity of a problem that will not yield quickly. I am still in the middle of this chapter, and I do not wish to hurry out of it. Milan has given me no final answer. It has given me a better question, and enough light to keep walking with it.
