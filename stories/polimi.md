# Politecnico di Milano

`M.Sc. Telecommunication Engineering · Milan, Italy · 2024–2026`

<img align="right" width="250" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/Polimi/Polimi_1stBuilding.jpg" alt="Politecnico di Milano">

When I first came to Milan, I did not know how to call it an adventure. The word felt too clean, too confident. What I had was simpler: a suitcase, a few careful documents, and the old habit of measuring distance before trusting it. I had come from the mountains of Gansu, through the long discipline of Beijing, and now into a city where even the air seemed to speak before I did.

The first days were small and exact. A tram bell in the morning. A receipt folded into my pocket. The pause before ordering coffee. The slow work of reading a street sign, then reading it again. I remember the tiredness of living in translation, not only between languages, but between versions of myself. One part of me wanted to become new quickly. Another part kept looking back, as if the road behind me might disappear if I stopped remembering it.

Milan did not ask me to solve this. It simply let me stay. I walked the same streets until the corners grew familiar. I learned which light appeared after rain, which bakery opened early, which path to campus was kindest when my thoughts were heavy. Belonging did not arrive like a door opening. It arrived like water warming in the hand: slowly, almost secretly, until I noticed I was no longer cold.

I have always been easily wounded by beauty, and just as easily healed by it. A clean equation can do this. So can a roof at evening, or a line of trees, or the first glimpse of mountains when the mind had expected only buildings. In Milan this weakness became part of ordinary life.

Sometimes the Alps appeared at the end of a street, pale and still, like a thought too large to finish. Lake Garda opened so widely that I could not decide whether I was standing before water or before a different kind of sky. The Duomo changed with the hour. In the afternoon it seemed not so much made of stone as slowly persuaded into light.

I do not think beauty makes difficulty smaller. That would be too easy. But it gives difficulty a place to rest. On days when research would not move, when English felt clumsy in my mouth, when homesickness came without warning, I could look at the city and feel that the world had not withdrawn its tenderness. It was still there, quiet and exact, asking nothing from me except attention.

PoliMi has taught me a different kind of attention. In engineering, a thing cannot be understood by admiration alone. A system may look elegant and still be hiding its cost. A result may be correct and still be shallow. One has to ask the quieter questions: where does the time go, what assumption is being protected, which part waits while another part works?

My thesis lives in that space. I study communication for large-scale AI training, where many GPUs must learn together across distance. From far away, distributed training can sound almost abstract: models, clusters, bandwidth, throughput. Up close, it becomes more intimate. Machines wait for one another. Information moves, stalls, gathers, scatters. Computation is strong, but it is not free from the need to speak.

I often think there is a small philosophy hidden in this problem. What does it mean for many separate parts to move as one? What is lost in the waiting between them? How much of intelligence depends not on the brilliance of a single unit, but on the patience and speed of its exchanges? These questions belong to systems, but they also belong to people who cross countries and languages and try, slowly, to remain whole.

My English has changed here. At first it was a room where I stood carefully, afraid to disturb the furniture. Now I move inside it with more ease. I argue, write, hesitate, correct myself, begin again. It has not erased the earlier rooms of my life. It has opened windows between them.

I am still a melancholic person. I still carry weather inside me. But Milan has made that weather less lonely. It has shown me that sadness can be a form of listening, that curiosity can be a way of staying alive, and that a life does not have to become simple in order to become more spacious. For now I am still here, between code and light, between distance and belonging, trying to ask better questions of both machines and myself.
