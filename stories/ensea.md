# ENSEA · A Brief Bright Detour

`Erasmus exchange · Networks, Telecommunications & Security · Cergy, France · Sep 2025 – Jan 2026`

<img align="right" width="345" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/ENSEA/ENSEA_snowday.jpg" alt="A snow day at ENSEA">

France entered my timeline after Milan had already begun to feel less impossible. It did not replace the road. It opened a side door in it.

My semester at **ENSEA** was brief, but memory does not measure life by duration alone. Some months pass and leave almost nothing. Others remain like a small lamp in the mind, lit not because they were perfect, but because they gave a different shape to the same journey.

Cergy was quieter than Milan. The air felt more spacious, the campus more contained, as if the world had lowered its voice so I could hear my own thoughts again. I arrived with the ordinary uncertainties of exchange life: documents, luggage, housing, schedules, names I would need time to remember. I had crossed countries before, but each crossing asks its own price. You are never foreign in exactly the same way twice.

Then snow came. It settled on the roofs and paths, softening the edges of the school. For a morning the campus seemed to speak gently. There was no grand revelation. Only the mild astonishment of being far from home and still finding the world capable of tenderness.

<br clear="all">

<img align="left" width="215" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/ENSEA/LaGrange_ENSEA.jpg" alt="La Grange, home for my first week">

The beginning could have been difficult. I did not yet have a permanent room. **Damien**, a classmate from the student union, welcomed me into his family's old house, La Grange, with the ease of someone doing the obvious thing. I still think about that ease. To the guest, a small kindness can feel enormous; to the person offering it, it may be only Tuesday.

Damien was friendly in a way that made a foreign place less theatrical and more livable. He organized parties, brought people together, and once told me my English carried an Italian accent. I liked that remark more than I expected. It meant I had already been changed by one country before entering another. Even my pronunciation had become a small map of where I had been.

There was a stone cellar beneath La Grange. At night it filled with music, low light, laughter, French moving too quickly for me, English arriving like a bridge, and the strange relief of being included before I had earned it. I followed conversations with my face as much as with my words. I understood late. I smiled late. No one seemed impatient with this.

That week taught me that hospitality is not only a feeling. It is a practice. A chair is moved. A sentence is repeated. A door is left open. Someone waits while you search for the word. The soul, when it is far from home, notices these small arrangements and remembers them for years.

<br clear="all">

ENSEA gave the academic part of the semester a clear rhythm. A network was first a drawing, then an address, then a trace, then a failure that had to be understood. The lab did not care whether I sounded confident. It answered only to attention.

In that sense, France continued the same education that had begun in Beijing and widened in Milan. BIT taught me that invisible things can be measured. PoliMi taught me that large systems live or die by communication. ENSEA placed those ideas into a compact, practical frame: networks as living arrangements of delay, trust, loss, routing, recovery. Not formulas on a page alone, but agreements between machines about how to keep meaning alive while moving.

This is the logic I began to love. Information is never simply "sent." It is shaped, protected, delayed, interpreted, sometimes damaged, sometimes recovered. A signal, a packet, a sentence, a memory — each survives only through a path. Perhaps that is why communication engineering feels close to life for me. Nothing important arrives without being carried.

One of the first classmates I met in my ENSEA program was **Ewan**, whose family came from Tunisia generations before. He was kind, serious, and quietly excellent in the way I admire most: not performative, not loud, but steady. On Christmas Day in 2025, I visited his home. It became one of those days that does not need a large plot to become unforgettable. Warm rooms, shared food, a family's ordinary generosity, the feeling of being allowed, for a few hours, to belong to someone else's shelter from winter.

<br clear="all">

But France was not only school. In the first days, **Janus** took a group of international students through Paris. We moved through the city like people walking inside a sentence they had once read in a dream. We took a boat on the Seine. The light of that northern latitude touched my face with a strange familiarity, as if some earlier version of me had already been there before I was born.

Paris had once been one of the places I imagined as a child when I did not yet know how far the world was. To stand there for real felt almost impossible. Not triumphant, exactly. More fragile than triumph. I remember thinking that a life can sometimes exceed the imagination that first saved it.

I remember walking with classmates through the Tuileries Garden. The gravel made a dry, gentle sound under our shoes. The trees stood in their deliberate lines, as if nature had agreed, for an afternoon, to think in geometry. We were talking about nothing urgent. That may be why I remember it so clearly. Happiness rarely announces itself while it is happening. It simply walks beside you and lets you understand later.

I remember Cergy lake even more privately. I had water plants in my hand, wet and green, and I threw them back into the lake. The surface opened, received them, and closed again. It was almost nothing. No photograph would have known what to do with it. But my fingers remember the cool stems, the slight resistance before letting go, the rings widening on the water until the lake became still.

Such moments do not ask to become symbols. They ask only to have existed. Yet they stay because they answer a question we rarely know how to ask: what makes a life feel real while it is passing?

At night, on the RER, I watched houses move through the dark. Each window held a square of warm light. I imagined, without ambition, a future made of such a window: a desk, a lamp, a quiet room, a life I could return to without performing. France gave me that image gently. Not certainty. Not success. A possible peace.

When I left ENSEA, the semester did not feel finished. It felt folded into the larger road. Beijing had given me endurance. Milan had given me distance and a wider intellectual sky. Cergy gave me softness — the proof that becoming serious about one's work does not require becoming hard.

That may be the quietest lesson of this chapter. A person can keep moving and still remain tender. A mind can become sharper without becoming colder. And sometimes, between one city and the next, the world places a small lamp in your hand so you can see the road not farther, but more gently.
