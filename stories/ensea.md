# ENSEA · Cergy, France

`Erasmus exchange — Networks, Telecommunications & Security · Sep 2025 – Jan 2026`

<img align="right" width="360" src="../assets/photos/ENSEA/ENSEA_snowday.jpg" alt="A snow day at ENSEA">

Even now, my exchange semester at ENSEA reads back to me like something I dreamed rather than lived. I can scarcely believe I was there at all — a boy from a far corner of China, set down one autumn in a small French town he had never heard of, watching the snow settle over a campus that was never meant to be part of his story. Some doors you don't so much choose to walk through as fall through, gratefully, at the exact moment you happen not to be looking.

<br clear="all">

<img align="left" width="245" src="../assets/photos/ENSEA/LaGrange_ENSEA.jpg" alt="La Grange, home for my first week">

It began, fittingly, without a roof over my head. For my first week a classmate from the student union simply took me in — his family's old house, La Grange, with a low stone cellar beneath it where we threw parties deep into the night and danced until the very walls seemed to breathe. I have never forgotten a single hour of that week. It felt like a tale from another century — some village boy improbably carried across the world and welcomed at a stranger's table — and I have quietly suspected, ever since, that very few people ever gather a life as strange, and as lucky, as this one. The whole term ran on that same current of wonder.

<br clear="all">

ENSEA taught in a way I had never met before: the grande-école model, severe and exacting, built on the plain assumption that you will rise to meet it. I loved every difficult hour. I spent the term deep among networks — their theory and their laboratories — and I leaned, without a shred of apology, on GPT, the patient and tireless tutor that so many of us can no longer quite do without.

But what I came to love most was the town itself, that quiet place northwest of Paris. I can still see the RER sliding toward the city in the early dark, row upon row of little houses slipping past the window, each one holding its own small lit square of warmth against the winter. I would sit with my forehead nearly against the glass and make myself a quiet promise — that one day, somewhere, one of those lit windows would be mine.
