# Beijing Institute of Technology · 2019–2023

`B.Sc. Electronic Information Engineering · Beijing, China`

<p align="justify">I arrived in Beijing in the autumn of 2019 to study <strong>Electronic Information Engineering</strong> at <strong>Beijing Institute of Technology</strong>. Before Beijing, there was Gansu: mountains, long distances, the Yellow River, festival colors in winter streets, and a childhood that taught me to notice every small opening carefully.</p>

<p align="justify">Beijing was larger than any place I had known. At first it felt dry, fast, and difficult to read. I had entered university through examinations, so I was used to clear targets: a score, a ranking, a gate crossed. The city did not work like that. It asked me to build a rhythm before I could feel steady inside it.</p>

<p align="justify"><a href="../assets/photos/BIT/DSC_6534.JPG"><img align="right" hspace="16" width="220" src="../assets/photos/BIT/DSC_6534.JPG" alt="BIT campus by the water" title="BIT campus by the water"></a>The lake, the buildings, the path back to the dormitory, the long campus evenings — none of them became meaningful all at once. They became familiar slowly, by repetition. A place first looks like a map; then, after enough ordinary days, it begins to feel like memory.</p>

<br clear="all">

## Home in Gansu

<p align="justify"><a href="../assets/photos/BIT/Yellow_River_Lanzhou.jpg"><img align="right" hspace="16" width="220" src="../assets/photos/BIT/Yellow_River_Lanzhou.jpg" alt="The Yellow River in Lanzhou" title="The Yellow River in Lanzhou"></a>Before Beijing, home was not an abstract origin. It had a river, winter festivals, mountain light, and streets where memory felt close to the ground. I did not carry those places as decoration; I carried them as a measure of distance. They made Beijing feel enormous at first, but they also made every new step more concrete.</p>

<p align="justify">The colors of Gansu were very different from the gray speed of Beijing. Shehuo, the Yellow River, and the dry air of Lanzhou belonged to a world where tradition was not a museum word; it was sound, clothing, dust, and people standing close together in the street. I think that is why I was always sensitive to place. A city was never only a city to me. It was light, texture, weather, and the way a person learns to breathe inside it.</p>

<br clear="all">

## Finding a rhythm at BIT

<p align="justify">The first year was mostly a process of learning how to live in a much larger environment. A dorm room became familiar. Then a library seat. Then the path to class, the canteen at certain hours, the winter air around Liangxiang, and the campus lights after evening study.</p>

<p align="justify">Those details mattered because they turned a huge city into a place I could actually move through. University life was not only lectures and exams. It was also repetition: carrying books, waiting for dinner, debugging assignments, returning to a difficult chapter the next morning. That repetition slowly gave me a sense of order.</p>

<p align="justify">The buildings themselves also taught a kind of discipline. Their clean lines, repeated windows, and quiet corridors made the day feel structured even when my own thoughts were not. I was still learning how to be independent, how to ask questions without feeling ashamed, and how to let a difficult course remain difficult for a while without giving up on it.</p>

<p align="justify">Academically, BIT gave me the language I needed for questions I had carried for a long time. I was drawn to physics because it asked what the world was doing beneath ordinary appearances. Electronic and information engineering made that curiosity practical. Signals, fields, circuits, probability, and communication systems showed me that invisible things could be measured, shaped, transmitted, and recovered.</p>

<p align="justify">I liked the discipline of it. A signal is not meaningful because we wish it to be. It has to survive noise, loss, delay, and imperfect hardware. A system either works, fails, or reveals the part we did not understand. That clarity suited me. It made curiosity less vague and more responsible.</p>

<p align="justify">Beihu in winter felt almost like a diagram of that clarity: little movement, hard air, a surface made quiet by cold. It was not dramatic, but it gave the mind room to become still.</p>

## Learning by building

<p align="justify"><a href="../assets/photos/BIT/BIT_CNC_Machine_Code_Programming.jpg"><img align="right" hspace="16" width="100" src="../assets/photos/BIT/BIT_CNC_Machine_Code_Programming.jpg" alt="CNC programming at BIT: handwritten instructions and the machine control panel" title="CNC programming at BIT: handwritten instructions and the machine control panel"></a>Some of the best learning happened when code had to interact with a physical device. Alongside coursework, I worked with machine programming, an Arduino car, and a Raspberry Pi. These projects brought together the parts of engineering that are easy to study separately: instructions, wiring, electronics, and the device itself.</p>

<p align="justify">The CNC photograph shows handwritten programming notes beside the machine's control panel. The same kind of instructions appears on paper and on the screen, connecting the programming exercise to the equipment that executes it. It is a concrete reminder of the step between writing code and using it to control a machine.</p>

<p align="justify">That connection was what interested me in practical work. A program could look reasonable on paper, but working with equipment also meant checking how it was entered, how the hardware was connected, and what the device actually did. It gave me another way to approach the material I was learning in class.</p>

<br clear="all">

<p align="justify"><a href="../assets/photos/BIT/BIT_Dragon_Warrior_Arduino_Car_Overview.jpg"><img align="right" hspace="16" width="220" src="../assets/photos/BIT/BIT_Dragon_Warrior_Arduino_Car_Overview.jpg" alt="The Dragon Warrior Arduino car: controller, sensors and wiring" title="The Dragon Warrior Arduino car: controller, sensors and wiring"></a>The Dragon Warrior Arduino car brought several components together on one chassis. In the photograph, the controller, ultrasonic sensor, wheels, and wiring are all visible. It shows the project as a connected system rather than just a finished enclosure: the electronics and their connections remain part of the picture.</p>

<p align="justify">Working with a small vehicle made the relationship between software and hardware more tangible. The code was one part of the project; the controller, sensors, power connections, and mechanical assembly were equally present. This was the side of electronics I enjoyed exploring alongside the more mathematical parts of my degree.</p>

<br clear="all">

<p align="justify"><a href="../assets/photos/BIT/RaspberryPie.jpg"><img align="right" hspace="16" width="130" src="../assets/photos/BIT/RaspberryPie.jpg" alt="Working with a Raspberry Pi" title="Working with a Raspberry Pi"></a>The Raspberry Pi was another part of that hands-on learning. Small experiments often came down to an ordinary debugging problem: a connection to check, a configuration to revisit, or a program behaving differently from what I expected.</p>

<p align="justify">These projects helped me develop a more patient way of working. I learned to narrow a problem, change one thing at a time, and check the result before moving on. They were modest exercises, but the habit carried over into later simulations and research work.</p>

## Beyond the classroom

<p align="justify">There were also days when Beijing softened. Yuyuantan in spring was one of them: open sky, blossoms, the central tower behind branches, and the feeling that the city could briefly stop demanding so much. After enough coursework and pressure, a walk through a park was not an escape from study; it was a way to remember that the world was wider than deadlines.</p>

<p align="justify">The blossom photos stayed with me because they were simple. Nothing important happened. I walked, looked, ate something small, and returned to ordinary life. But sometimes those are the days that keep a period from becoming only pressure in memory.</p>

<p align="justify">That small meal after walking now feels like part of the same story as the equations and experiments. It reminds me that a student life is never made only of achievement. It is also made of weather, hunger, tired feet, and brief moments when the mind unclenches.</p>

<p align="justify">The pandemic affected most of my undergraduate years. It changed schedules, closed gates, interrupted plans, and made PCR tests part of ordinary student life. There were repeated periods of being sealed inside campus or at home, and the uncertainty became tiring in a way that is hard to explain neatly.</p>

<p align="justify">The most difficult part was not one single event. It was the repetition: restrictions, openings, new rules, waiting, another test, another changed plan. Then in December 2022, everything changed suddenly. Looking back, that period still feels unreal. It took away some of the freedom that should have belonged naturally to those years.</p>

<p align="justify">Beijing outside the campus still moved with a huge technical and commercial energy. Streets, company buildings, buses, screens, and traffic all seemed to say that the world was accelerating, even while our student years were often interrupted and confined. That contrast was difficult to forget.</p>

## The undergraduate thesis

<p align="justify">In the final year, my undergraduate thesis focused on <strong>multi-node cooperative directional transmission in wireless ad hoc networks</strong>. The goal was simple to state but demanding to build: in a network without fixed infrastructure or a central base station, several nodes try to coordinate their transmissions so that their signals add up in a useful direction instead of spreading energy everywhere.</p>

<p align="justify">The work had two main parts. First, the nodes needed accurate time and frequency synchronization; otherwise, cooperation would only create disorder. Then, after synchronization, the transmission parameters could be adjusted through feedback so that a directional beam gradually formed. Most of my experiments were MATLAB simulations, including different node layouts and feedback strategies for distributed beamforming.</p>

<p align="justify">By the hot, dry end of May, after several months of work, I finally completed the thesis. I remember the lab near sunset: equipment on the table, simulation figures waiting to be checked, data to be saved, small errors still asking for attention. It was not a cinematic moment, but it was a clear one. A long task had reached its end, and I could see how much my way of thinking had changed since I first arrived in Beijing.</p>

<p align="justify">I left BIT with a stronger technical foundation, but also with a habit that mattered even more: when something is unclear, do not rush to decorate it. Measure it, question it, simplify it, and understand where the difficulty really is.</p>

<p align="justify">There were brief sparks outside the strict rhythm of study, too. They did not erase the difficult years, but they kept the memory from becoming flat. The undergraduate period was not clean or easy. It was uneven, interrupted, and sometimes cold to look back on; still, it taught me endurance, technical patience, and the first real shape of my own intellectual life.</p>
