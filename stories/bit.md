# Beijing Institute of Technology

`B.Eng. Electronic and Information Engineering · Beijing, China · 2019–2023`

<img align="right" width="340" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/BIT/DSC_6534.JPG" alt="BIT campus by the water">

I came to Beijing in the autumn of 2019 from a much smaller world. Gansu had given me mountains, dust, long distances, and the early knowledge that life is not evenly distributed. Some children inherit ease. Others learn, very young, to make a narrow road wider by walking it again and again.

For years I had moved forward through examinations. One gate, then another. A score, a ranking, a threshold crossed. This way of living made the world seem stern but understandable. Beijing broke that simplicity at once. The city did not feel like a gate. It felt like a weather system.

Roads circled roads. Buildings cut the sky into pieces. People moved with a certainty I could not share. In those first months I often felt like a page torn from one book and slipped into another, unable to find the sentence around me.

<br clear="all">

<img align="left" width="330" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/BIT/GongxunLou.jpg" alt="Gongxun Building, BIT">

BIT gave me a slower entrance into that vastness. First there was the dorm room, small but mine in the way a borrowed corner can become mine. Then a seat in the library. Then the path to class, the canteen at a certain hour, the winter air around Liangxiang, campus lights returning one by one at dusk.

None of these things was heroic. That is why they mattered. A life becomes believable through repetition. You buy dinner. You carry books. You sit with a difficult problem. You wake the next morning and continue. Little by little, the city remains large, but you no longer vanish inside it.

Beijing did not make me fearless. It made me steadier. That was a quieter gift, and perhaps a more useful one.

<br clear="all">

<img align="right" width="330" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/BIT/DSC_6609.JPG" alt="BIT campus architecture">

At BIT, physics and engineering began to meet inside me. Physics was the question I had carried since childhood: what is the world doing when no one is looking? Engineering was the hand placed gently on that question, asking whether the invisible could be measured, shaped, transmitted, made to answer.

Signals fascinated me because they were both real and hidden. A waveform could pass through copper, air, noise, mathematics, and still arrive with meaning. Fields, filters, transforms, probability - each subject gave me a different lamp. I began to feel that the world was not less mysterious because it had laws. It was more mysterious, because the laws were there at all.

I did not only want to pass courses. I wanted to touch the thing behind them. A circuit was not merely a requirement. A line of code was not merely an assignment. A noisy measurement was not merely a problem to clean. Each was a way of asking: what can be known, and how carefully must we listen before the answer appears?

<br clear="all">

<img align="left" width="340" hspace="18" vspace="4" src="../assets/photos/BIT/Tsinghua.jpg" alt="A drone at Tsinghua">

The hours I loved most often happened after the official day had ended. A breadboard under a desk lamp. A Raspberry Pi refusing to behave for no noble reason. A program failing for an hour because of one small thing I could not see. Then the tiny relief: the LED blinking, the data appearing, the motor turning, the circuit answering back.

I still remember the laboratory where I finished my undergraduate thesis. It was an evening near the end. The sun was going down, and the room was filled with that soft, tired gold that makes even instruments look briefly human. Wires lay across the table. The screen gave off its pale, practical light. Outside, Beijing was lowering itself into dusk.

For a moment everything was painfully real. The work. The room. The years behind me. The younger self who had once only wanted to escape the narrowness of his beginnings. Nothing dramatic happened. The data still had to be saved. Someone would eventually turn off the lights. Yet I felt that if truth had a temperature, it might have been close to that evening: warm, fading, impossible to hold.

The pandemic passed through most of my undergraduate years. Time became strange. Plans changed, rooms emptied, gates closed and opened, and the future often looked as if someone had covered it with a grey cloth. I do not want to make those years noble simply because they are over. They were lonely. Sometimes they were only hard.

But the world kept leaving small evidence that it had not abandoned us. Beihu under snow. The outline of Gongxun Building at dusk. Roommates returning late with food. A laptop humming after midnight. A concept becoming clear after days of resistance. These things did not solve the darkness. They sat beside it.

I arrived in Beijing trained to answer questions placed before me. I left BIT beginning to ask my own. That change was quiet, but it remains one of the most important changes of my life. Whenever I work now, some part of me returns to that laboratory evening: the wish to make thought touch the real world, and the knowledge that the moments which form us cannot be kept, only carried.
